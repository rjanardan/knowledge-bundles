# Don Valentine

The founder of Sequoia Capital, who came to venture capital from semiconductor sales and built the firm on a market-first thesis that outlasted him by decades.[^wiki-dv]

## Snapshot

* Born 26 June 1932 in New York City, raised in the Bronx in a working-class Catholic family; died 25 October 2019.[^wiki-dv]
* Graduated from Fordham University, chosen because it was affordable and close to home and paid for quarterly in cash; he later credited the Jesuit education with the Socratic questioning style he became known for.[^wiki-dv]
* First came to California during military service after college and stayed.[^wiki-dv]
* Sales engineer at Raytheon, then a senior sales and marketing executive at National Semiconductor, then worldwide sales at Fairchild Semiconductor.[^wiki-dv]
* Founded Sequoia Capital in 1972 in Menlo Park; the firm's first investment was Atari in 1975, and it invested 150,000 dollars in Apple in 1978.[^wiki-dv]
* Backed, through Sequoia, companies including LSI Logic, Oracle, Cisco, Electronic Arts, Google and YouTube.[^wiki-dv]

## Education

* BA from Fordham University. No graduate degree is recorded in the sources read.[^wiki-dv]

## Career and outcomes

* His semiconductor years put him in continuous contact with early-stage chip customers, where he began informally assessing young companies — the origin of his investing.[^wiki-dv]
* His account of timing was inflection points: the most transformative startups emerged where technological disruption opened a market to new entrants.[^wiki-dv]
* Sequoia's first investment, Atari, came in 1975 and was sold to Warner Communications for 28 million dollars; he met Steve Jobs while Jobs was a line engineer at Atari, which led to Sequoia's investment in Apple.[^wiki-dv]
* The 150,000 dollars Sequoia put into Apple in 1978, alongside Atari and the later positions in Oracle and Cisco, became the reference points for early-stage technology investing in the United States.[^wiki-dv]
* In the mid-1980s downturn he insisted on disciplined spending and rigorous evaluation, and the firm avoided the speculative excesses that damaged others.[^wiki-dv]

## Contributions

* His investment thesis, as the sources read describe it: markets rather than founders dictate a company's ultimate scale, so the firm should look for large markets early, then for companies in the "confusion phase" where incumbents have not worked out how a new technology reshapes them.[^wiki-dv]
* He preferred founders who "knew what they didn't know" — intellectual humility and adaptability over charisma — and was known for blunt, demanding board meetings built on Socratic questioning of assumptions and financial discipline.[^wiki-dv]
* He demanded brevity: the sources record him requiring one entrepreneur to rewrite an entire proposal onto the back of a business card, and he was sceptical of hype cycles such as the early "information highway" rhetoric.[^wiki-dv]
* He preferred to back companies based in Northern California, which is one explanation for the concentration of the firm's early portfolio in the Bay Area.[^wiki-dv]
